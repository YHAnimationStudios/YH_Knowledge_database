## Assistant

    **Blender can produce convincing close-up destruction. For our B-17, I’d use pre-cut skin animated with bones and shape keys, exported to Unreal as a Skeletal Mesh.** This also offers a way to preserve the hull’s three UV channels: UE’s skeletal FBX pipeline supports multiple UV sets and morph targets. [Epic documentation](https://dev.epicgames.com/documentation/unreal-engine/fbx-skeletal-mesh-pipeline-in-unreal-engine).
    
    The practical workflow would be:
    
    1. **Extract the damage area.** Work on a copied fuselage or wing patch, retaining its exterior UVs and material assignments. Include the frame/stringers that will become visible.
    
    2. **Model the tear before animating it.** Cut irregular seams and create separate metal flaps. Initially, their edges meet in the intact position; animation pulls them apart. Shape keys move existing vertices—they cannot create new holes or split topology during playback. [Blender shape-key workflow](https://docs.blender.org/manual/id/5.2/animation/shape_keys/workflow.html).
    
    3. **Add thickness and exposed edges.** Give the skin an inner surface and visible rim, predominantly inward to preserve the exterior silhouette. Assign exposed metal separately. Finalize cuts, thickness, and subdivision before creating shape keys.
    
    4. **Combine bones and sculpted shapes.**
       - **Bones:** large rotations, peeling, curling, and eventual panel departure.
       - **Shape keys:** dents, sharp creases, buckling, and irregular edge deformation.
       
       A useful progression is **dent → seam opens → flaps curl → selected pieces detach**. Bones handle curling better than interpolating directly between two widely different shapes.
    
    5. **Bake and export the animation.** Bring the skeleton and morph targets into UE, rebind the existing materials, and play the animation in Sequencer. Blender’s FBX exporter warns that **Apply Modifiers prevents shape-key export**, so prepare the topology beforehand and disable that option when exporting morphs. Test a small patch first. [Blender exporter](https://docs.blender.org/api/main/bpy.ops.export_scene.html), [UE morph import](https://dev.epicgames.com/documentation/unreal-engine/fbx-morph-target-pipeline-in-unreal-engine).
    
    For additional procedural movement, **cloth simulation can help with loose-panel flutter**, using pinned attachment areas and tuned stiffness/damping. I would use it as secondary motion: convincing permanent metal folds still need deliberate shaping. Blender documents pinning and animated rest-shape controls for this purpose. [Cloth controls](https://docs.blender.org/manual/en/4.5/physics/cloth/settings/shape.html).
    
    **Alembic remains an option** for complicated baked deformation, but our earlier finding still applies: standard UE Geometry Cache conversion retains only UV0. Skeletal animation is therefore my preferred first test for this particular aircraft.
    
    We can still automate variation—tear outlines, flap sizes, deformation amounts, and timing—around reusable prepared panels. The tradeoff is more authored setup than a dedicated metal-destruction solver.
    
    I’d start with **one fuselage patch showing a puncture, several peeling flaps, and exposed structure**, then combine that technique with Chaos for detached rigid debris.
